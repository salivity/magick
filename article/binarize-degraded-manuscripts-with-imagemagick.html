Binarize Degraded Manuscripts with ImageMagick

Binarizing degraded historical manuscripts requires specialized image processing to separate faded or damaged ink from uneven, discolored parchment. Standard global thresholding frequently fails on historical documents due to bleed-through, stains, and non-uniform lighting. This guide outlines the most effective ImageMagick methods—specifically Local Adaptive Thresholding and Background Division Normalization—to produce high-contrast, clean binary text images from degraded sources.

The Local Adaptive Thresholding Method

The simplest native ImageMagick approach to handle varying lighting and background stains across a manuscript is Local Adaptive Thresholding (-lat). Unlike standard thresholding, which uses a single global cutoff value for the entire image, -lat calculates a local threshold for each pixel based on the average intensity of the surrounding neighborhood.

The syntax for -lat is:

magick input.jpg -colorspace Gray -negate -lat WIDTHxHEIGHT+OFFSET% -negate output.png
  • WIDTHxHEIGHT: Defines the pixel size of the local analysis window. This should be significantly larger than the stroke width of the text (typically between 25x25 and 50x50).
  • OFFSET%: Sets the sensitivity offset from the local mean. A typical value ranges between 5% and 15%.
  • -negate: Used before and after because -lat identifies light objects on dark backgrounds by default; negating ensures dark text on light parchment is extracted properly.

The Background Division Normalization Method

For heavily degraded documents with severe aging, foxing, or ink bleed-through, Background Division Normalization produces superior results. This method estimates the manuscript's uneven background illumination, divides the original image by that background model to flatten the lighting, and then applies a standard threshold.

Execute the process using the following command pipeline:

magick input.jpg -colorspace Gray \
  \( +clone -blur 0x25 \) \
  -compose Divide_Src -composite \
  -auto-level \
  -threshold 80% \
  output.png

How the Pipeline Works:

  1. -colorspace Gray: Converts the manuscript into a single grayscale channel to prepare for tonal adjustments.
  2. \( +clone -blur 0x25 \): Creates an in-memory duplicate and applies a heavy Gaussian blur. The large blur radius eliminates the text strokes while preserving the macro-level illumination gradients, shadows, and stains.
  3. -compose Divide_Src -composite: Divides the original image by the blurred background. This normalizes the background to near-white across the entire document while keeping the text dark.
  4. -auto-level: Stretches the tonal range of the normalized image from pure black to pure white to maximize contrast.
  5. -threshold 80%: Applies a final binary cutoff. Pixels darker than the specified percentage become solid black text, while the rest turn pure white.

Noise Reduction and Text Enhancement

Historical manuscripts often retain small background speckles or broken character strokes after binarization. You can refine the output by adding morphological cleanup steps directly into the processing pipeline:

magick input.jpg -colorspace Gray \
  \( +clone -blur 0x30 \) \
  -compose Divide_Src -composite \
  -auto-level \
  -threshold 75% \
  -morphology Open Disk:1 \
  output.png
  • -morphology Open Disk:1: Performs an erosion followed by a dilation, which removes isolated single-pixel noise (salt-and-pepper artifacts) without altering overall text legibility.
  • -morphology Close Disk:1: If the ink is broken or faint, closing can bridge small micro-gaps within individual letter strokes.
