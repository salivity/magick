Difference Between CIELab and CIELuv in ImageMagick

Both CIELab and CIELuv are device-independent, perceptually uniform color spaces supported by ImageMagick to isolate lightness from chromatic information. While both define lightness (\(L^*\)) identically, they diverge in their chromatic coordinate calculations, resulting in CIELab being optimized for surface and reflective colors like print, whereas CIELuv is mathematically tailored for additive lighting and self-luminous displays.

The Mathematical Origin and Design

The International Commission on Illumination (CIE) established both standards simultaneously in 1976 to address non-linearities in the original CIE 1931 XYZ space.

  • CIELab (\(L^*a^*b^*\)): Designed primarily to model human vision based on the opponent-color theory. It uses three axes: \(L^*\) represents lightness (from 0 for black to 100 for white), \(a^*\) represents the red-green opponent axis, and \(b^*\) represents the yellow-blue opponent axis. Its mathematical formulation applies cube-root transformations to XYZ values, providing a uniform representation for subtractive color models and dye/pigment behaviors.
  • CIELuv (\(L^*u^*v^*\)): Formulated as a projective transformation of the CIE 1931 chromaticity diagram (\(u^\prime, v^\prime\)). It retains the exact same \(L^*\) metric for lightness as CIELab, but its chromatic axes (\(u^*\) and \(v^*\)) describe chromaticity differences relative to a reference white point based on uniform chromaticity scale (UCS) coordinates.

Implementation in ImageMagick

In ImageMagick, both color spaces can be invoked directly via the -colorspace operator:

# Convert to CIELab
magick input.png -colorspace Lab lab_output.png

# Convert to CIELuv
magick input.png -colorspace LUV luv_output.png

ImageMagick stores both spaces in three channels:

  • Channel 0: Lightness (\(L^*\)) in both spaces.
  • Channel 1: \(a^*\) in CIELab; \(u^*\) in CIELuv.
  • Channel 2: \(b^*\) in CIELab; \(v^*\) in CIELuv.

Because image file formats generally store pixel data as unsigned integers normalized between 0 and the quantum range (e.g., 0 to 255 for 8-bit, 0 to 65535 for 16-bit), ImageMagick offsets and scales the chromatic values (\(a^*/b^*\) and \(u^*/v^*\)) to fit into valid pixel data ranges when saving to disk.

Additive Color Mixing and Straight Lines

The primary functional difference appears when performing color interpolation or simulation:

  • CIELuv preserves straight lines for additive mixtures. Because \(u^*\) and \(v^*\) derive directly from CIE UCS projective transformations, additive mixtures of two colored lights lie along a straight line on the chromaticity diagram. This makes CIELuv mathematically superior in ImageMagick for operations simulating color displays, LED outputs, or lighting effects.
  • CIELab curves additive mixtures. In CIELab, additive mixtures of two lights do not fall on a straight line. The cube-root transformations cause curvature, particularly in saturated blues and purples.

Perceptual Uniformity and Image Comparison

Both spaces aim for perceptual uniformity, meaning a Euclidean distance metric (\(\Delta E\)) represents an equal perceived visual difference anywhere in the color gamut:

  • Color Difference (\(\Delta E\)): When calculating differences using ImageMagick's -metric comparison tool, CIELab is the standard foundation for industry-standard metrics like \(\Delta E^*_{76}\), \(\Delta E^*_{94}\), and \(\Delta E_{00}\). ImageMagick relies heavily on CIELab for perceptual image comparisons and delta computations.
  • Tone Curve Adjustment: If you separate the \(L^*\) channel to sharpen an image or apply contrast modifications without shifting hues, both CIELab and CIELuv yield almost identical lightness results because their \(L^*\) channels are calculated using the exact same formula.

Summary of Use Cases

  • Use CIELab in ImageMagick when preparing assets for physical print workflows, simulating pigments, or computing standardized \(\Delta E\) image difference metrics.
  • Use CIELuv in ImageMagick when manipulating colors meant for digital screens, additive lighting simulations, or when straight-line chromaticity interpolation between light sources is required.
