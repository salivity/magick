EWA Resampling vs Orthogonal Scaling in ImageMagick

Generalized Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) resampling in ImageMagick provides superior anisotropic filtering and eliminates moiré artifacts during image transformation, but it introduces a substantial computational penalty compared to traditional orthogonal scaling. While standard orthogonal scaling applies two fast, separable 1D passes across image axes, generalized EWA evaluates an unconstrained 2D elliptical footprint for every destination pixel. Understanding the exact computational trade-offs between these two methods requires analyzing algorithmic complexity, per-pixel matrix operations, and the dramatic performance divergence that occurs during downscaling.

Algorithmic Complexity: Separable vs. Non-Separable Filtering

The primary source of the performance difference lies in filter dimensionality:

  • Orthogonal Scaling (\(O(2R)\)): Standard resizing operations (such as -resize) use separable filters. Scaling is split into two independent 1D passes: first horizontally along the rows, then vertically down the columns. If a reconstruction filter has a support radius of \(R\), computing an output pixel requires sampling \(R\) source pixels in the horizontal pass and \(R\) source pixels in the vertical pass, leading to an algorithmic complexity of \(O(R + R) = O(2R)\) operations per pixel.
  • Generalized EWA Resampling (\(O(R^2)\)): Generalized EWA (used in operations like -distort) is a true 2D, non-separable filter. Instead of separate linear passes, ImageMagick reconstructs each output pixel by integrating an arbitrary elliptical area in source space. Evaluating this ellipse requires probing a 2D bounding box, resulting in a complexity of \(O(R_x \times R_y) \approx O(R^2)\) operations per pixel.

As filter support radius increases, the \(O(R^2)\) complexity of EWA scales quadratically, whereas orthogonal filtering scales linearly.

Per-Pixel Computational Overhead

Beyond the raw sample count, EWA imposes significantly higher mathematical overhead for every sample evaluated:

  1. Mapping Derivatives: EWA must calculate partial derivatives (the Jacobian matrix) at every destination pixel to determine local scaling, shearing, and rotation in source space.
  2. Ellipse Geometry Formulation: The Jacobian matrix is combined with the filter's covariance matrix to establish the coefficients of an implicit bivariate quadratic equation: \(Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 = F\).
  3. Weight Computation: For every candidate pixel within the bounding box, ImageMagick evaluates this quadratic form to determine whether the point lies inside the ellipse and to compute its radial distance for weight lookup. Orthogonal scaling bypasses these matrix evaluations entirely, relying on simple 1D coordinate offsets and pre-calculated weight tables.

The Impact of Downscaling

The performance gap between orthogonal scaling and generalized EWA is most pronounced during heavy minification (downscaling):

  • In orthogonal downscaling, the 1D filter radius expands proportionally to the reduction factor \(S\). The number of samples evaluated per pixel grows linearly: \(2 \times (R \cdot S)\).
  • In generalized EWA downscaling, both axes of the elliptical footprint expand proportionally to \(S\). The area of the ellipse—and consequently the number of pixel lookups required—grows quadratically: \(\pi \times (R_x \cdot S) \times (R_y \cdot S) \propto S^2\).

Downscaling an image by a factor of 4 increases the orthogonal sample footprint by approximately \(4\times\), whereas the EWA sample footprint expands by approximately \(16\times\).

Practical Performance Cost

In real-world ImageMagick implementations, generalized EWA resampling via -distort is typically 5 to 30 times slower than orthogonal scaling via -resize. When performing 1:1 affine mappings or subtle scaling, EWA often runs 3x to 8x slower due to matrix overhead and cache locality limits. Under severe downsampling or extreme perspective transformations where the projected ellipse encompasses large swaths of the source image, the processing time of EWA can exceed that of orthogonal scaling by two orders of magnitude unless clamped by limiting parameters (such as filter:blur or bounding maximum support).
