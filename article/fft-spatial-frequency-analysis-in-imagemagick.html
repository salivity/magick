FFT Spatial Frequency Analysis in ImageMagick

This article provides a practical guide on performing spatial frequency analysis using ImageMagick’s Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) tools. You will learn how to decompose an image into its frequency domain components, visualize and interpret the magnitude spectrum, apply frequency filters to isolate or remove specific patterns, and reconstruct the final image using the Inverse Fast Fourier Transform (IFT).

Understanding the FFT Process in ImageMagick

ImageMagick processes an image in the spatial domain (pixels) and transforms it into the frequency domain using the -fft operator. This operation produces two separate grayscale images:

  1. Magnitude Component (Index 0): Represents the strength or amplitude of spatial frequencies. Low frequencies (smooth gradients, broad areas) gather at the center, while high frequencies (edges, fine details, noise) spread toward the outer edges.
  2. Phase Component (Index 1): Encodes the structural alignment and spatial location of the frequencies. Both components are required to accurately reconstruct the original image.

Generating the Frequency Spectrum

To compute the forward FFT on an image, run the following command in your terminal:

magick input.png -fft fft_output_%d.png

This generates two files: fft_output_0.png (Magnitude) and fft_output_1.png (Phase). ImageMagick automatically centers the zero-frequency (DC) component in the magnitude image.

Visualizing the Magnitude Spectrum

Because the magnitude spectrum has a high dynamic range with massive values concentrated at the center, the raw output image often appears entirely black with a single bright dot. To make the full frequency distribution visible for analysis, apply a logarithmic transformation and normalize the levels:

magick fft_output_0.png -evaluate log 10000 -auto-level magnitude_visible.png

In the resulting image:

  • Center brightness: Represents the overall average brightness (DC component) and low frequencies.
  • Radial distance from center: Corresponds to increasing spatial frequency.
  • Directional spikes or dots: Represent periodic noise, lines, or repeating grid patterns oriented perpendicular to the spike direction.

Filtering Spatial Frequencies

To alter or remove features in the spatial domain, modify the magnitude image while keeping the phase image intact.

1. Removing Periodic Patterns (Notch Filtering)

If an image contains repetitive scanlines or grid artifacts, they appear as distinct bright dots off-center in the magnitude spectrum. Paint over these specific dots with black (value 0) in an image editor or using an overlay mask to eliminate the frequency.

2. Low-Pass Filtering (Smoothing)

To retain only low frequencies and blur high-frequency details, mask out the outer regions:

magick fft_output_0.png \( -size 512x512 radial-gradient:white-black \) -compose Multiply -composite filtered_magnitude.png

(Note: Adjust the dimensions to match your input image.)

3. High-Pass Filtering (Edge Isolation)

To remove flat surfaces and isolate edges, attenuate the center values by inverting the radial mask before multiplying.

Reconstructing the Image (Inverse FFT)

After modifying the magnitude component, recombine it with the original phase component using the -ift operator to convert the data back into a viewable spatial image:

magick filtered_magnitude.png fft_output_1.png -ift reconstructed.png

The resulting reconstructed.png reflects your frequency-domain adjustments in the spatial domain.
