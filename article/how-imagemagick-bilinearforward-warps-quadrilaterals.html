How ImageMagick BilinearForward Warps Quadrilaterals

This article explains how ImageMagick’s -distort BilinearForward operator maps an arbitrary quadrilateral into a rectilinear grid. It covers the underlying mathematical formulas, how forward-defined mapping is inverted during pixel synthesis, and how this technique differs from true perspective projection when normalizing distorted planes.

The Mathematical Framework

When using the BilinearForward distortion operator, ImageMagick defines the relationship between the source image coordinates \((u, v)\) and the destination image coordinates \((x, y)\) using a pair of bilinear polynomial equations:

\[x(u, v) = c_0 u + c_1 v + c_2 u v + c_3\] \[y(u, v) = c_4 u + c_5 v + c_6 u v + c_7\]

To map a quadrilateral to a rectilinear grid, the user supplies four pairs of coordinate points representing the four vertices of the source quadrilateral and the four corresponding corners of the destination rectangle. These four coordinate pairs yield eight independent equations, allowing ImageMagick to solve for the eight unknown coefficients (\(c_0\) through \(c_7\)) via linear algebra.

The defining characteristic of this model is the \(u v\) cross-term. While linear terms handle scaling, translation, and shearing, the cross-term allows opposite edges of the source quadrilateral to non-parallelly diverge or converge, accommodating arbitrary non-rectangular quadrilateral shapes.

Inverse Mapping and Pixel Lookup

Graphics renderers cannot reliably push pixels from source to destination because gaps, moiré artifacts, and overlaps can appear in the output. Instead, ImageMagick uses an inverse-mapping (pull) method for rasterization.

Even though the transformation is specified as BilinearForward—meaning the destination coordinates are defined as explicit functions of the source coordinates—ImageMagick must calculate which source coordinate \((u, v)\) corresponds to each integer pixel \((x, y)\) on the destination rectilinear grid.

To achieve this:

  1. The destination is configured as a rectilinear grid aligned with the pixel raster.
  2. For each destination pixel \((x, y)\), ImageMagick algebraically inverts the forward equations.
  3. Solving for \(u\) and \(v\) from the forward system requires solving a quadratic equation, which generally produces two mathematical roots.
  4. ImageMagick selects the valid root that falls within the geometric bounds of the source quadrilateral.
  5. Once the fractional source coordinate \((u, v)\) is determined, ImageMagick applies an Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) filter (or the selected -filter setting) to sample and reconstruct the source area into the destination pixel.

Geometric Behavior: Bilinear vs. Perspective

Transforming a quadrilateral to a rectangle with BilinearForward creates a distinctly different result than a projective -distort Perspective transformation:

  • Grid Line Curvature: In a perspective transformation, straight lines in 3D space remain straight lines when projected onto a 2D plane. In bilinear distortion, straight lines connecting opposite edges remain straight only if they are parallel to the boundary axes; diagonal lines across the quadrilateral bend into curves across the surface.
  • Lack of Foreshortening: Perspective transformations model optical depth by dividing coordinates by a depth factor \(W\) (homogeneous coordinates). Bilinear mapping does not account for depth or vanishing points; it enforces uniform parametric interpolation across edges.
  • Surface Shape: Bilinear mapping mathematically models the deformation as a doubly ruled surface (a hyperbolic paraboloid). When mapping a distorted quad back to a rectilinear grid, the pixels are stretched uniformly relative to the perimeter edge ratios rather than compressing with simulated distance.
