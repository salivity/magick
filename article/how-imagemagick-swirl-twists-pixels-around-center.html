How ImageMagick Swirl Twists Pixels Around Center

This article explains the underlying mechanics of ImageMagick's -swirl operator, detailing how it transforms two-dimensional Cartesian coordinates into a rotational vortex around a specific focal point. You will learn how the transformation calculates angular displacement based on radial distance, how inverse pixel mapping prevents graphical artifacts, and the methods used to position the swirl's center at custom coordinates rather than the default canvas center.

Coordinate Space and Center Points

By default, ImageMagick evaluates the center of the swirl effect at the exact midpoint of the target image:

\[\left(x_c, y_c\right) = \left(\frac{\text{width}}{2}, \frac{\text{height}}{2}\right)\]

Because the native -swirl operator takes only an angular degree argument (-swirl <degrees>) without native coordinate flags, shifting the swirl center requires defining an active sub-region or padding the canvas. Using the -region WxH+X+Y option creates a localized bounding box whose center acts as the vortex anchor. Alternatively, manipulating the canvas with -extent or -repage allows you to align any user-defined coordinate with the operational center before applying the effect.

The Polar Coordinate Transformation

The algorithm converts each pixel's Cartesian coordinates \((x, y)\) relative to the chosen center \((x_c, y_c)\) into polar coordinates \((r, \theta)\):

  1. Distance Calculation:
    \[r = \sqrt{(x - x_c)^2 + (y - y_c)^2}\]

  2. Current Angle:
    \[\theta = \arctan2(y - y_c, x - x_c)\]

The transformation sets an operational radius (\(R\)), which typically defaults to the shortest or longest distance from the center to an edge, depending on the implementation version.

Distance-Dependent Angular Displacement

The degree of twist is not uniform across the image; it decays progressively as the distance from the center increases. The angle of rotation added to each pixel is weighted by a linear or cosine falloff factor relative to the operational radius \(R\):

\[\text{factor} = 1.0 - \frac{r}{R}\]

When \(r \ge R\), the factor becomes zero, leaving outer pixels unaltered. For pixels inside the radius (\(r < R\)), the new angle \(\theta'\) is calculated as:

\[\theta' = \theta + \text{degrees} \times \left(\frac{\pi}{180}\right) \times \text{factor}\]

Pixels closest to \((x_c, y_c)\) receive the maximum rotational displacement matching the full degree argument. As distance approaches \(R\), the rotational shift smoothly tapers off to eliminate harsh boundaries between distorted and undisturbed areas.

Inverse Mapping and Interpolation

ImageMagick utilizes inverse mapping (pulling source pixels to target locations) rather than forward projection (pushing source pixels to target coordinates). Forward projection often causes visual artifacts, such as pinholes or overlapping pixel conflicts.

For every coordinate \((x', y')\) in the output buffer:

  1. The displacement formula is reversed to compute the exact original location \((u, v)\) from which that pixel's color data must be drawn.
  2. Because the calculated \((u, v)\) values are rarely whole integers, ImageMagick queries its filter engine to interpolate surrounding pixels.
  3. The configured -interpolate setting (such as bilinear, bicubic, or catrom) samples the adjacent integer source coordinates to generate a smooth, anti-aliased pixel value at the destination point.
