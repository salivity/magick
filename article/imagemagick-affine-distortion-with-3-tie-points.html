ImageMagick Affine Distortion with 3 Tie Points

This article explains how ImageMagick’s -distort Affine operator converts three pairs of source and destination coordinates (tie points) into a geometric transformation. By mapping three points from an input image to three points on an output canvas, ImageMagick constructs a system of linear equations to solve a 2D affine transformation matrix. This matrix encapsulates translation, rotation, scaling, and shearing without requiring separate mathematical operations for each property.

The 2D Affine Transformation Model

An affine transformation maps parallel lines to parallel lines and preserves ratios of distances along lines. Mathematically, the mapping of an input coordinate \((u, v)\) to an output coordinate \((x, y)\) is represented by a set of linear equations:

\[x = c_{00}u + c_{01}v + c_{02}\] \[y = c_{10}u + c_{11}v + c_{12}\]

In matrix notation, this is expressed using homogeneous coordinates:

\[\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c_{00} & c_{01} & c_{02} \\ c_{10} & c_{11} & c_{12} \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

The coefficients \(c_{02}\) and \(c_{12}\) govern translation (\(t_x, t_y\)), while the \(2 \times 2\) submatrix composed of \(c_{00}, c_{01}, c_{10},\) and \(c_{11}\) encodes scaling, rotation, and shear.

Solving the System with Three Tie Points

A single tie point provides one source coordinate \((u_i, v_i)\) and one target coordinate \((x_i, y_i)\), yielding two linear equations. Three non-collinear tie points provide six independent equations:

  1. \(x_0 = c_{00}u_0 + c_{01}v_0 + c_{02}\)
  2. \(y_0 = c_{10}u_0 + c_{11}v_0 + c_{12}\)
  3. \(x_1 = c_{00}u_1 + c_{01}v_1 + c_{02}\)
  4. \(y_1 = c_{10}u_1 + c_{11}v_1 + c_{12}\)
  5. \(x_2 = c_{00}u_2 + c_{01}v_2 + c_{02}\)
  6. \(y_2 = c_{10}u_2 + c_{11}v_2 + c_{12}\)

Because there are six equations and exactly six unknown coefficients (\(c_{00}\) through \(c_{12}\)), ImageMagick solves this system directly using standard matrix inversion or Gaussian elimination. Provided that the three source points do not form a straight line (which would cause a singular matrix with a determinant of zero), the solution yields a unique affine matrix.

Extracting Scaling, Rotation, and Shear

ImageMagick does not need to decompose the matrix to distort the image; the matrix itself defines the complete transform. However, the geometric components are mathematically embedded in the \(2 \times 2\) submatrix:

\[M = \begin{bmatrix} c_{00} & c_{01} \\ c_{10} & c_{11} \end{bmatrix}\]

This matrix can be decomposed into elementary transformations:

\[M = R(\theta) \cdot S(s_x, s_y) \cdot H(\gamma)\]

Where:

  • \(R(\theta)\) is a rotation matrix.
  • \(S(s_x, s_y)\) is a non-uniform scaling matrix.
  • \(H(\gamma)\) is a shear matrix.

1. Calculating Scale Factors (\(s_x, s_y\))

The scale factors represent how basis unit vectors are stretched:

  • The x-axis scale factor is the Euclidean norm of the first column:
    \(s_x = \sqrt{c_{00}^2 + c_{10}^2}\)
  • The y-axis scale factor is calculated after removing the projection along the transformed x-axis, or directly via the matrix determinant:
    \(s_y = \frac{\lvert c_{00}c_{11} - c_{01}c_{10} \rvert}{s_x}\)

2. Calculating Rotation (\(\theta\))

The rotation angle is determined by the direction of the transformed x-axis: \[\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(c_{10}, c_{00})\]

3. Calculating Shear (\(\gamma\))

Shearing occurs when the angle between the transformed basis vectors deviates from 90 degrees. It can be derived from the dot product of the column vectors normalized by scale: \[\gamma = \operatorname{atan2}(c_{00}c_{01} + c_{10}c_{11}, s_x^2)\]

Backward Mapping and Image Resampling

Once the matrix coefficients are computed, ImageMagick inverts the matrix:

\[\begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c_{00} & c_{01} & c_{02} \\ c_{10} & c_{11} & c_{12} \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

ImageMagick uses this inverse matrix to map each destination pixel \((x, y)\) back to its corresponding source coordinate \((u, v)\). It then applies Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) resampling to reconstruct the final pixel color, ensuring that scaling, rotation, and shear are rendered without aliasing artifacts.
