ImageMagick EWA Resampling in Non-Affine Distortions

This article explains how ImageMagick applies Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) resampling to produce sharp, alias-free results during non-affine image distortions. When executing non-linear transformations such as perspective, polar, barrel, or polynomial mappings, the pixel footprint deforms dynamically across the surface. ImageMagick handles this by calculating local derivative matrices for each pixel, mapping circular output footprints into source ellipses, and performing weighted integration through specialized radial filter lookups.

The Challenge of Non-Affine Mapping

In affine transformations, the scaling, rotation, and shearing factors remain uniform across the entire image, meaning a single transformation matrix applies to all pixels. Non-affine distortions—such as perspective projections, bilinear grids, and lens corrections—feature continuously varying scales and orientations across the plane. A uniform filter size would cause severe blurring in stretched regions or harsh aliasing (moiré patterns) in compressed regions. EWA resampling solves this by adapting the reconstruction filter's size, shape, and orientation on a per-pixel basis.

Step 1: Inverse Mapping and Local Linearization

ImageMagick processes distortions using an inverse mapping approach. Instead of pushing source pixels onto the target grid, it iterates through each destination pixel \((x, y)\) and determines the corresponding coordinate \((u, v)\) in the source image.

Because the mapping function \(f(x, y) = (u, v)\) is non-linear in non-affine distortions, ImageMagick linearizes the transformation locally at each destination pixel coordinate using partial derivatives:

\[J = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \\ \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} \end{bmatrix}\]

Depending on the distortion type:

  • Analytic Derivatives: For well-defined geometric functions (such as perspective or affine), ImageMagick evaluates exact mathematical formulas for the partial derivatives at \((x, y)\).
  • Numerical Approximation: For complex or free-form mappings, ImageMagick approximates the partial derivatives using finite differences by sampling adjacent pixel offsets: \((x + \delta, y)\) and \((x, y + \delta)\).

Step 2: Formulating the Resampling Ellipse

In the destination space, a single pixel's sampling area is typically treated as a unit circle. When mapped backward through the Jacobian matrix \(J\), this circular footprint transforms into an ellipse in the source image space.

ImageMagick constructs the quadratic form of the ellipse equation:

\[Q(u', v') = A u'^2 + 2B u'v' + C v'^2\]

Where \((u', v')\) are coordinates relative to the central mapped point \((u_0, v_0)\). The coefficients \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\) define the shape, eccentricity, and orientation of the ellipse:

  • If the image is contracted (minification), the ellipse expands in source space to gather more pixels, preventing aliasing.
  • If the image is enlarged (magnification), the ellipse shrinks. To prevent the ellipse from collapsing into a zero-radius singularity—which would cause interpolation artifacts—ImageMagick imposes a minimum filter radius (often clamping to standard reconstruction filters like Robidoux, Mitchell, or Gaussian).

Step 3: Determining the Bounding Box

Evaluating every source pixel for every destination pixel is computationally prohibitive. ImageMagick limits processing by computing an axis-aligned bounding box around the source ellipse.

Using the coefficients \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\), the algorithm determines the maximum extents:

\[u_{max} = \sqrt{\frac{C \cdot F^2}{AC - B^2}}, \quad v_{max} = \sqrt{\frac{A \cdot F^2}{AC - B^2}}\]

Here, \(F\) represents the cutoff radius of the chosen filtering kernel (such as the support radius of a Gaussian or cylindrical Lanczos function). ImageMagick then loops exclusively over integer source coordinates within the range:

\[[u_0 - u_{max}, u_0 + u_{max}] \times [v_0 - v_{max}, v_0 + v_{max}]\]

Step 4: Radial Weight Calculation and Accumulation

For every integer coordinate \((u, v)\) inside the bounding box, ImageMagick computes the elliptical distance metric:

\[r^2 = A(u - u_0)^2 + 2B(u - u_0)(v - v_0) + C(v - v_0)^2\]

If \(r^2 \le F^2\), the point lies within the footprint of the filter:

  1. Weight Lookup: The value \(r^2\) is scaled to index into a precomputed one-dimensional lookup table containing the radial weighting filter values (often a windowed Jinc/Bessel or Gaussian function).
  2. Value Accumulation: Color channels (and alpha channel values) are multiplied by the retrieved weight and added to a running sum.
  3. Weight Normalization: The total accumulated color values are divided by the total accumulated weight, ensuring energy conservation and preventing unintended brightness shifts.

Through this combination of local Jacobian approximation, ellipse projection, and normalized radial weighting, ImageMagick provides seamless anti-aliasing across surfaces with severe perspective compression and non-linear stretching.
