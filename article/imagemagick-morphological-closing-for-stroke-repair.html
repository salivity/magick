ImageMagick Morphological Closing for Stroke Repair

This article explains how ImageMagick repairs broken, fragmented line art and text strokes using mathematical morphology. By applying a morphological "Close" operation, ImageMagick bridges micro-fractures, fills small gaps, and restores continuity to damaged strokes without permanently altering the general thickness or geometry of the shapes.

The Problem of Damaged Strokes

Scanned documents, low-resolution rasterization, and harsh binarization often produce broken or discontinuous strokes in text and line drawings. These fractures disrupt optical character recognition (OCR) and degrade visual fidelity. Standard blurring or thickening can close these gaps, but it often leads to unwanted stroke weight expansion and loss of fine detail.

What is Morphological Closing?

Morphological closing is a two-step mathematical operation derived from set theory: dilation followed immediately by erosion, both using the same structuring element (kernel).

  1. Dilation (Expansion): The operation scans the binary or grayscale image and expands the foreground (strokes). As foreground pixels grow outward, adjacent stroke segments expand toward each other until their boundaries overlap, effectively bridging the fracture.
  2. Erosion (Contraction): The operation then contracts the foreground pixels by the exact same radius. While the overall stroke shrinks back to its original baseline thickness, the bridge formed across the fracture remains intact, provided the initial gap was smaller than the kernel diameter.

Because dilation and erosion cancel each other out on continuous boundaries, closing acts selectively: it fills internal holes and connects narrow breaks while leaving the outer dimensions of the stroke largely unchanged.

Implementing Closing in ImageMagick

ImageMagick provides access to morphological operations via the -morphology operator. The standard syntax for stroke reconstruction is:

magick input.png -morphology Close <KernelType>[:<Radius>] output.png

Selecting the Kernel

The structuring element determines the shape and reach of the connection:

  • Disk: The most common kernel for general stroke repair (e.g., Disk:1.5). It bridges gaps uniformly in all directions without introducing sharp corners.
  • Diamond: Useful for low-resolution or pixelated images (e.g., Diamond:1) to prevent excessive diagonal bridging.
  • Rectangle or Line: If breaks occur predominantly along a specific axis (such as horizontal scanning dropouts), directional kernels like Rectangle:3x1 can target horizontal gaps without affecting vertical spacing.

Controlling the Reconstruction

The kernel radius defines the maximum gap width that can be repaired. A kernel with a radius of \(R\) can bridge gaps up to \(2R\) pixels wide.

Selecting the appropriate size is critical:

  • Under-filtering: If the kernel is too small, the dilation phase will fail to make the broken stroke segments touch, and the subsequent erosion will simply restore the original disconnected state.
  • Over-filtering: If the kernel is too large, it may bridge intentional gaps, fusing distinct characters together (e.g., merging "c" and "l" into "d", or closing the loop in "e").

For best results on grayscale scans, images are typically thresholded or normalized prior to applying the closing operation, ensuring a clean distinction between the damaged stroke foreground and the background.
